Goosebumps happen when tiny muscles under your skin - called arrector pili - pull on your hair follicles, triggered by your body's fight-or-flight system.
It's not just cold that sets them off. Fear, awe, and a powerful moment in a song can trigger the exact same reflex.
It's a leftover from when humans had way more body hair - puffing it up once made our ancestors look bigger to predators and trapped warmth.
These days, with barely any body hair left, goosebumps do almost nothing for keeping you warm. Mostly, they're just leftover wiring.
That chill down your spine during a favorite song has an actual name: frisson. Scientists study it as a real, measurable response.
In 2020, Harvard researchers found the same muscle behind goosebumps also anchors the nerves that keep hair follicles alive - so this "useless" reflex might be secretly running your hair growth.
