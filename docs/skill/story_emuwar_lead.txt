True story: The Great Emu War, Western Australia, November-December 1932.

Setup: After World War One, farmers in Western Australia were struggling through the Great Depression. Then roughly 20,000 emus migrated inland during their breeding season and started tearing through wheat farms, trampling fences and eating crops. Many of the farmers were WWI veterans themselves, so they asked the government for machine guns to deal with it.

Escalation: The government agreed and sent three soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery, led by Major G.P.W. Meredith, armed with two Lewis machine guns and 10,000 rounds of ammunition. This was treated as a real military operation against the birds.

First failure: On November 2, 1932, the soldiers tried to ambush a flock near Campion. The emus scattered into small groups the instant the shooting started instead of staying bunched up, so the gunners could barely get more than a few shots off before losing their targets. Only about a dozen birds were killed in that first engagement.

Second failure, worse: A few days later they tried again at a dam where about 1,000 emus had gathered - a much better setup. But one of the machine guns jammed almost immediately, and the birds scattered again before more than a handful could be killed.

Why they kept losing: Emus can run up to 45 kilometers per hour (28 mph), zig-zag unpredictably, and have tough hides that let a lot of glancing bullets bounce off without killing them. Major Meredith himself compared them to Zulu warriors in how well they could absorb hits and keep moving.

The final numbers: After about a month of this, the official tally was 9,860 rounds of ammunition fired for approximately 986 confirmed emu kills - suspiciously exactly ten bullets per bird. Meredith actually reported this as a good ratio, though many suspected the real number of dead birds was much lower and he was covering for the operation's failure.

The twist/payoff: The whole thing became a national and international embarrassment. It was raised in the Australian Parliament, mocked in newspapers around the world, and the military was pulled out for good. Farmers ended up dealing with the emus themselves using bounty systems instead. To this day it's remembered as the time Australia's own army officially lost a war against a flightless bird.
