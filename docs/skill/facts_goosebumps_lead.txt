	#6. Goosebumps (piloerection) happen when tiny arrector pili muscles under the skin contract and pull on hair follicles, triggered by the autonomic ("fight or flight") nervous system. Cleveland Clinic, Popular Science.
	#5. Cold isn't the only trigger -- fear, awe, and a powerful moment in music can set off the same reflex, via the amygdala (the brain's emotional center) signaling the autonomic nervous system. Popular Science, ScienceNewsToday.
	#4. It's an evolutionary leftover from when humans had far more body hair -- puffing the hair up once made ancestors look bigger to predators and helped trap warm air, the same mechanism still visible in many mammals today.
	#3. In modern humans, with much less body hair, goosebumps do almost nothing for actual temperature regulation anymore -- it's largely a vestigial reflex. HEDGE: "largely vestigial for warmth" is the mainstream framing, not an absolute -- some minor thermoregulatory contribution isn't fully ruled out, but the consensus is it's negligible in humans specifically.
	#2. The chill/shiver during a powerful moment in a song has a real scientific name -- frisson -- and is a studied, measurable phenomenon, not just a figure of speech.
	#1 (payoff, the stance beat). A 2020 Harvard study (Shwartz, Gonzalez-Celeiro, Chen, Pasolli et al., published in Cell) found the same arrector pili muscle that causes goosebumps also physically anchors the sympathetic nerves that keep hair-follicle stem cells active -- meaning the "useless" goosebump reflex may double as part of the wiring that controls hair growth, with potential relevance to baldness research.
