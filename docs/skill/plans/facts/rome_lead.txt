VERIFIED FACTS:
- Circus Maximus: Guinness credits it as the largest stadium ever built,
  ~255,000 seated spectators (other sources give a wider 150,000-300,000
  range depending on era/method of counting — used the Guinness figure as
  the single citable number). ~6x the Colosseum's ~50,000 capacity.
- Roman road network: over 400,000 km total, ~80,500 km paved. The Appian
  Way (Via Appia), begun 312 BCE, is the first major paved Roman road and
  sections remain walkable today.
- Gladiators: modern historians (analysis of inscriptions, Pompeii graffiti
  outcome records, Georges Ville's study of 1st-century CE bouts) estimate
  roughly 1 in 5 fights ended in the loser's death — i.e. ~80% of defeated
  gladiators survived. Economic reason: gladiators were trained, valuable
  property, expensive to replace. (Noted but not scripted: this shifted
  toward more lethal fights by the 3rd century CE as public taste changed —
  omitted from the script for space, not because it contradicts the fact.)
- Vomitorium: genuinely just the passage/exit under stadium seating that lets
  crowds "spew forth" (Latin vomere) at the end of an event — coined by the
  5th-century writer Macrobius for this literal architectural meaning. The
  "room where Romans purged between courses" idea has no ancient evidence;
  first documented mistaken use traced to Aldous Huxley's 1923 novel "Antic
  Hay." Genuine myth-debunk, not a hedge.
- Colosseum naval battles (naumachiae): Cassius Dio explicitly describes a
  sea battle staged in the Colosseum in 86 CE with the arena flooded deep
  enough to float ships. However, there is no surviving physical evidence,
  and modern historians are genuinely split — some propose the accounts
  describe smaller "theatrical" naumachia (shallow water, small boats) rather
  than full-scale battles. Scripted with an explicit hedge ("historians still
  debate how dramatic it really was") rather than stated as flatly settled,
  matching the channel's convention for genuinely contested claims.
- Roman self-healing concrete: MIT/Harvard team (led by Admir Masic),
  published in Science Advances, January 2023. Confirmed that "lime clasts"
  (small white lumps long assumed to be a sign of sloppy mixing) are a
  deliberate result of "hot mixing" — combining quicklime directly with
  volcanic ash and water at high heat. When a crack reaches a clast, water
  dissolves calcium from it and recrystallizes inside the crack, sealing it.
  This is the leading explanation for why 2,000-year-old Roman harbor
  structures still stand while modern concrete typically degrades within
  decades. Actively being studied to bring similar self-healing concrete to
  real-world use (e.g. seawalls).
