Because a frog's eyes and nostrils sit on top of its head, it can float almost completely submerged in water while still seeing and breathing normally, with only its eyes and nose poking above the surface.
Frogs can breathe partly through their skin, absorbing oxygen directly from the water around them, which is part of why they can stay nearly motionless at the surface for long stretches.
Frogs have a third eyelid called a nictitating membrane, a thin translucent layer that slides sideways across the eye, letting them keep watching their surroundings for danger even while the eye stays protected and moist underwater. Do not say frogs "hunt" underwater with this eyelid -- frogs mainly catch prey with a tongue-flick at the water's edge or on land, not by actively hunting while submerged; the eyelid's role is protection and moisture while swimming or resting underwater, not hunting.
When a frog swallows food, it blinks and pulls its own eyeballs down into its skull; the eyeballs push against the roof of its mouth from inside and physically shove the food down its throat.
Frogs do not drink water through their mouths at all. Instead they absorb it directly through a patch of specialized skin on their belly and inner thighs called a "drinking patch" or "pelvic patch."
A group of frogs has its own unusual name: a group of frogs together is called an army, one of the odder collective nouns in the animal kingdom.
