VERIFIED FACTS:
- All ~18 living penguin species are native exclusively to the Southern
  Hemisphere (Antarctica, sub-Antarctic islands, South Africa, South America
  including the Galápagos, Australia, New Zealand). None are native to
  Greenland or anywhere in the Arctic.
- Penguin ancestors evolved in the Southern Hemisphere roughly 60 million
  years ago. They are flightless, so they cannot cross the warm equatorial
  belt and the nutrient-poor tropical waters that separate the hemispheres —
  this, not temperature alone, is why they never spread north.
- THE TWIST: the word "penguin" was not coined for these birds. It was first
  used, centuries earlier, for the Great Auk (Pinguinus impennis) — a large,
  flightless, black-and-white, upright seabird of the North Atlantic.
  European sailors later encountered the superficially similar flightless
  birds of the Southern Hemisphere and called them "penguins" too, by
  resemblance to the auk, even though the two groups are not closely related
  (Great Auks belong to the auk/puffin family, Alcidae; true penguins are
  Sphenisciformes) — a case of convergent evolution, not common ancestry.
- The Great Auk's range included the North Atlantic waters around
  Newfoundland (its largest colony, ~10,000 pairs, on Funk Island), Iceland,
  Scotland, the Faroe Islands, and Norway. Circumstantial evidence (strong
  but not directly confirmed by a nest find) points to a breeding population
  in southwestern Greenland near Paamiut — i.e., Greenland very likely did
  have its own "penguin" once.
- The species was hunted for down, meat, oil, and later as museum/collector
  specimens, driving it to extinction. The last confirmed breeding pair was
  killed on the Icelandic island of Eldey on 3 June 1844; the pair's single
  egg was crushed in the process.
