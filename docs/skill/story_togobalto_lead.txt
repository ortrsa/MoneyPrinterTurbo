3	THE FACTS (independently verified):
	- January 1925, Nome, Alaska: a diphtheria outbreak threatened the town, and the only antitoxin serum was in Anchorage/Nenana, roughly 674 miles away by the fastest available route.
	- A relay of roughly 20 mushers and 150+ sled dogs carried the serum across Alaska in a blizzard, temperatures reported around -40°F to -50°F with wind chill.
	- Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog Togo (12 years old at the time, near typical retirement age for a sled dog) ran the longest and most dangerous leg of the relay: 264 miles round-trip, including a deliberate shortcut across the frozen, unstable ice of Norton Sound during a whiteout blizzard -- other teams in the relay averaged about 31 miles each.
	- The final 55-mile leg into Nome was run by musher Gunnar Kaasen and his lead dog Balto. Balto's team arrived in Nome first (February 2, 1925) and received the public credit -- Balto became a national celebrity, the subject of a movie, and got a statue erected in New York's Central Park later that year.
	- Seppala publicly felt Togo was denied the credit he deserved. Most dog-sledding historians and experts today agree Togo's leg was the objectively harder, more dangerous, and more consequential one, both in distance and conditions.
