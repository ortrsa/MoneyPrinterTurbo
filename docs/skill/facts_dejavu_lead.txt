3	ALL 6 FACTS INDEPENDENTLY WEB-VERIFIED:
	#1. "Deja vu" is French for "already seen." Most people experience it at least once in their life. HEDGE: exact prevalence figures vary a lot by study (one source cited "up to 97% at least once, 67% regularly" -- a single-source figure, not independently replicated across the board) -- script uses the conservative, safely-true "most people, at least once" framing rather than quoting a specific percentage.
	#2. Frequency peaks around ages 15-25 (one study: ~2.5x/year average in that range) and then declines with age -- counterintuitive since most memory-related phenomena get MORE common with age, not less. Leading explanation: it may reflect strong neural "fact-checking"/conflict-detection ability that's sharper when young and weakens with age, not a memory failure itself.
	#3. The term was first used in French scientific literature in 1876, by philosopher Emile Boirac (a letter in Revue Philosophique). NOTE (not used in the script, just documented for completeness): the term reportedly went unused again until 1893 (Andre Lalande) and was formally proposed at a Paris medical-psychology society meeting in 1896 -- kept the spoken fact simple ("first used... 1876...Boirac") since the full multi-step history is more detail than a single spoken sentence needs and doesn't change the core fact.
	#4. People with temporal lobe epilepsy report deja vu far more often as a seizure "aura" -- and neurologist Wilder Penfield showed decades ago (1950s-90s follow-on research) that directly, electrically stimulating the temporal lobe (specifically near the perirhinal cortex, more reliably than the hippocampus) can trigger the exact deja vu sensation in conscious patients during brain surgery.
	#5. Leading scientific theory: a "familiarity signal" in the brain's perirhinal cortex misfires/fires without an actual matching memory behind it, making something genuinely new feel oddly familiar. HEDGE APPLIED: framed as "the leading theory," explicitly not proven/settled -- multiple competing theories exist (memory-processing "short circuit" theories, attention/"double-processing" theories) and researchers themselves say deja vu is hard to study in a lab, so no single explanation has full consensus.
	#6 (payoff). A 2025 study (published in Consciousness and Cognition) tested whether people could actually predict what happens next during a deja-vu-like sense of familiarity (VR navigation task) -- they could NOT predict better than chance, despite the strong subjective feeling that they "know what's coming." The eerie "premonition" feeling is an illusion of prediction, not real precognition. HEDGE: this is one specific study's finding about predictive ability, not a claim that deja vu has been fully explained or that the feeling itself isn't real -- the feeling is real, the accuracy of the "prediction" isn't.
