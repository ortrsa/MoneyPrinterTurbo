In 1910, Alfa Romeo was founded in Milan, Italy. Decades later, a talented driver
named Enzo Ferrari raced for Alfa Romeo and in 1929 founded Scuderia Ferrari, which
became Alfa Romeo's racing division. In 1938 Alfa Romeo absorbed the racing team
in-house as Alfa Corse, sidelining Enzo Ferrari; disagreements over this and over
creative control led him to leave in 1939. He went on to found his own company,
which became Ferrari as it is known today (first Ferrari-badged car in 1947).

Later, tractor manufacturer Ferruccio Lamborghini bought several Ferraris and was
unhappy with the clutch, which he found harsh and prone to failure. According to
the origin story Lamborghini's own company still tells (and which is widely
reported, e.g. by Car and Driver), when he raised this directly with Enzo Ferrari,
Ferrari dismissed the complaint and told him to stick to building tractors. This
snub is said to have pushed Ferruccio Lamborghini to build sports cars himself,
founding Automobili Lamborghini in 1963. Note for the script: frame this
specifically as "the story Lamborghini itself tells" rather than as an
independently verified transcript of the conversation -- no primary source records
the exact exchange, similar to how the Mercedes-Benz naming story was handled by
citing the company's own account rather than presenting it as flatly proven.

Lamborghini's sports cars gained traction, helped significantly by a brilliant
engineer, Horacio Pagani, who joined in 1983 as head of the composites department
and pushed hard for advanced carbon-fiber construction (notably the Countach
Evoluzione prototype). Lamborghini's owners at the time did not give him the
freedom or budget he wanted to build out a proper carbon-fiber division.
Disagreements over this led Pagani to leave Lamborghini in 1988 and eventually
found his own company, Pagani Automobili (est. 1992), which is the hypercar maker
known today for extreme use of carbon fiber.

Narrative shape for the script: this is a chain of the same story repeating --
each time a founder is dismissed or denied by the company he helped build, he
walks away and starts a rival that becomes just as legendary. Alfa Romeo's loss
became Ferrari. Ferrari's insult became Lamborghini. Lamborghini's refusal became
Pagani. The reveal/payoff should land on this pattern explicitly as the callback,
not just list the four companies.
